What the footage actually is

Measured from the sample clip rather than assumed. Several of these numbers changed the design.

Four thousand pixels on the long axis is a much larger budget than a typical phone clip. The rider spans roughly 790 px at drop-in and still 205 px at takeoff, which is enough for pose estimation throughout. That is the single most encouraging fact in the whole clip.

A brightness trace over a static patch shows a coefficient of variation of 0.4% with no dominant peak, so the floodlights are not beating against the 60 fps capture. Flicker correction is not needed.

02

The three phases

Phase is the organizing unit of the whole system. Metrics are computed per phase, compared per phase, and charted with phase bands. Analysis stops at takeoff.

≈ 3.95 – 4.55 s · rider 940 → 760 px

Drop-in

Rider enters from the right of frame, close to camera, travelling across the image rather than into it. Best resolution of the whole run and the most favourable geometry.

  • Initial stance and knee flexion at entry
  • Fore/aft CoM — board's long axis is across the frame here
  • Entry speed and line onto the mat
≈ 4.55 – 5.70 s · rider 760 → 330 px

Transition · edge change

The arc across the mat and the switch of edge. The rider's board rotates from across-frame to into-frame through this phase, so measurement quality degrades continuously rather than at a step.

  • Edge-change timing and duration
  • Compression and extension through the arc
  • Shoulder–hip separation building into the run-out
  • Fore/aft becomes progressively less trustworthy
≈ 5.70 – 6.45 s · rider 330 → 205 px

Takeoff run

Straight line to the lip. Board points down the optical axis, so the depth-aligned quantities are gone. Lowest resolution of the run, and the phase you most want to measure.

  • Final stance, trunk lean, arm position
  • Pre-pop compression and the extension into the lip
  • Line offset from the mat centreline
  • Fore/aft and board pitch unavailable
Nothing after the board leaves the lip is in scope. That simplifies the system considerably: no ballistic model, no airborne rotation tracking, no landing detection. Takeoff is a terminator, not a transition.

Fore/aft balance is not uniformly unavailable, as an earlier read of the still frame suggested. It is good at drop-in, degrades through the transition, and is gone by the takeoff run. Report it with the phase attached and suppress it in phase three rather than dropping it from the system.

04

Tool decisions

Four choices worth researching, two of which the footage revised.

Pose model

RTMPose over MediaPipe

Recent comparisons put RTMPose highest on overall accuracy, with MediaPipe fastest on processing speed. RTMPose, Sapiens and ViTPose are the most robust as conditions vary.

At 150 frames per run, throughput is irrelevant. Take the accuracy.

Revised

Take rtmlib, leave the rest

Sports2D wraps RTMLib neatly and computes joint and segment angles, but its pixel-to-metre step assumes motion in a plane parallel to the camera, stated as its own key limitation.

That fails here. Use rtmlib for keypoints, write the calibration and phase logic yourself.

Revised · night footage

The shadow is the real hazard

Floodlights cast a hard rider-sized shadow onto the pale matting. A quick background-subtraction pass on the clip latched onto it repeatedly, inflating bounding boxes to more than double the rider's height.

Detector on the person, never on the motion blob. Reject dark regions that lie flat on the mat plane.

Runtime

CoreML barely matters here

ONNX Runtime's CoreML provider needs macOS 10.15+ and prefers a Neural Engine, but it partitions the graph and falls back to CPU for unsupported ops, so a speedup isn't guaranteed.

150 frames will run in seconds either way. Benchmark it once and stop thinking about it.

05

The mat is your calibration target

Scale is not constant in this view, so a single pixels-per-metre number is meaningless. The matting solves it for free, and the static camera means you solve it once.

  • Ground homography. The dry-slope tiles form a dense, sharp, regular grid across the whole run, clearly resolved even in the far field. Four correspondences into cv2.findHomography gives an image-to-surface map for the entire mat.
  • Solve once per session. The camera does not move within a clip and, if the tripod position is repeatable, does not move between them. Store the homography against the session, not the clip.
  • Metric board track. Push the board contact point through cv2.perspectiveTransform for true position, speed and lateral drift, free of perspective.
  • Precision needs no calibration at all. Ground sampling in millimetres equals 1750 divided by the rider's pixel height, given a 1.75 m stature. Log rider pixel height per frame and every error bar follows from it.
  • Vertical scale separately. The ground homography says nothing about height. Recover it from the rider's stature at a known ground point.

Stabilisation, which mattered a great deal when a panning camera was assumed, is now a verification step rather than a stage. Confirm the background is locked, log the residual, and move on.

The pipeline

Seven stages, offline, one clip at a time. Two of them are new since the footage arrived.

01

Ingest

Read HEVC, apply the −90 rotation metadata explicitly rather than trusting the decoder, index frames against presentation timestamps.

PyAV
02

Trim to the run

Only a sixth of the clip contains a rider. Find the window by motion energy, pad it, and discard the rest before any model runs. Everything downstream gets six times cheaper for free.

OpenCV
03

Detect & track

Person detector rather than background subtraction, so the cast shadow is rejected by appearance. Crop with margin, upscale to model input, then pose. Log rider pixel height every frame.

rtmlib · ByteTrack
04

Segment phases

Drop-in, transition, takeoff run, from board yaw rate and path curvature on the mat plane. Every metric downstream carries its phase label and a validity flag.

Your module
05

Calibrate

Ground homography from the tile grid, solved per session. Vertical scale from stature. Error bars from rider pixel height.

OpenCV
06

Smooth & measure

Confidence-weighted gap filling, low-pass filter, then angles, centre of mass and board kinematics in surface coordinates. Never differentiate a raw signal.

SciPy
07

Store & present

Long-format metric rows to Parquet, session calibration and phase boundaries to SQLite, pre-rendered overlay video, JSON for the charts.

07

Data model and interface

Phase and validity travel with every value, so a chart can never show a number the geometry doesn't support.

metrics.parquet          # long format, one row per metric per frame
  clip_id      str
  frame_idx    int
  t_sec        float
  phase        str        # drop_in | transition | takeoff_run
  metric       str        # "com_height", "knee_flex_l", ...
  value        float
  confidence   float
  rider_px_h   float      # drives err_est
  err_est      float      # metric units
  valid        bool       # false where phase geometry kills it

catalog.sqlite
  sessions     (id, venue, camera_pose_hash, H_ground, v_scale)
  clips        (id, session_id, path, fps, run_start_s, run_end_s)
  phases       (clip_id, phase, t_start, t_end)

Interface

A local FastAPI backend and a plain HTML front end. A video element for playback, uPlot or Plotly for the charts, synchronised off the video's timeupdate event with one shared cursor. Colour the chart background by phase so the bands are always visible behind the curves.

Draw the error band from err_est everywhere, and grey out any series where valid is false rather than hiding it. A visibly suppressed fore/aft trace in the takeoff run teaches the geometry; a missing one just looks like a bug.

Because every run has the same three phases, the natural comparison view is phase-aligned overlay: pick two clips, warp each to a common phase timeline, and plot them together. That is more useful than aligning on wall-clock time and is the feature most likely to change how someone rides.

08

Capture notes

The current setup is better than expected. These are refinements, not fixes.

  • Keep the tripod position marked. A static, repeatable camera pose turns calibration into a once-per-season job and makes runs comparable across sessions.
  • A second camera beside the lip would restore fore/aft and board pitch for the takeoff run, the phase where they matter most and this view cannot supply them.
  • Shoot landscape if the framing allows. Portrait spends a lot of pixels on trees and gravel, though at 4K there is enough to go around.
  • Watch the shadow direction. If the floodlights can be arranged so the shadow falls behind rather than beside the rider, detection gets easier for free.
  • 120 fps if the phone offers it at 4K. Edge-change timing is the one measurement that would sharpen noticeably.
  • Start recording later. Six seconds of empty mat before the drop-in is harmless but wasteful; the trim stage handles it either way.
09

Build order

Get one real number you trust before building any pipeline around it.

  • Trim and run rtmlib on this clip. Overlay the skeleton across all 150 frames and watch where it degrades. That sets the honest working range before anything else is designed.
  • Build the ground homography by hand. Click four tile corners, transform the contact point, check the resulting speed is plausible for a jump approach.
  • Add phase segmentation. Board yaw rate is the signal. Verify the boundaries against your own eye on this clip.
  • Add CoM height and lateral offset. The first outputs that say something the eye cannot.
  • Build the phase-banded timeline UI against static JSON before wiring a backend.
  • Only then automate calibration, fine-tune a board detector, and add the phase-aligned comparison view.

The first two steps take an afternoon and will tell you whether the single-camera version is worth finishing, or whether a second camera at the lip should come first.
